Papers

  • Doing What Another Would Want [pdf]
    R&R Journal of Ethics and Social Philosophy

    We often do what another would want us to do, as when we continue a dead parent’s tradition, listen to a friend’s favorite musician, or channel a lover’s moral commitments. Such actions can be emotionally resonant and relationally significant. But if we have merely instrumental reasons to do what others would want given by the value of the underlying activity or of downstream outcomes, it’s unclear why these actions would have this significance. I introduce a paradigmatic form of doing what another would want I call “action from empathetic concern,” which requires, first, adopting an empathetic stance, and second, acting in light of the reasons that appear within that stance. By investigating our reasons for acting from empathetic concern, I explain why doing what another would want can be emotionally resonant: by valuing through another’s eyes and acting on those values, we respond to and strengthen our valuable relationships.

  • Preference Coalitions and the Ethics of Aggregation [pdf]
    Under review

    Some forms of aggregation, like intrapersonal aggregation, seem easier to justify than others. What conditions, in general, make aggregation easier to justify? Although many factors are plausibly relevant, this paper defends the Respectable Preference-Based Principle, on which aggregation is easier to justify when the affected parties respectably prefer it. Preferences are respectable, I argue, when they are informed, sufficiently reasonable, and sufficiently authentic. The view that emerges justifies aggregation by appealing not to impersonal value, but to personal value for the affected parties. This has some surprising upshots: intrapersonal aggregation is only sometimes fully justifiable; interpersonal aggregation can be even easier to justify than intrapersonal aggregation; and, in contrast with the popular idea that deontology covers people and utilitarianism covers animals, interanimal justification is in one important way harder to justify than interpersonal aggregation. The resulting view suggests a new way to draw moral lines, based not on the separateness of persons, but on the separateness of what I call “preference coalitions."

  • A Puzzle About Moral Status [pdf]
    Under review

    Any plausible moral theory will account for non-person animals. But do animals have the same moral status as people? Unitarianism – endorsed by Bentham, Singer, and DeGrazia – says ‘Yes.’ Hierarchism – endorsed by Vallentyne, Kazez, and Kagan – says ‘No.’ This paper introduces the following puzzle. Unitarianism and hierarchism seem to be the only possible views of animals’ moral status. But hierarchists argue unitarianism entails absurdly strong distributive reasons to benefit animals. And, I argue, hierarchism entails absurdly strong distributive reasons to benefit higher status superior beings, even those better off than typical people. The inevitable choice between unitarianism and hierarchism is thus a dilemma. Although, I argue, none of the direct responses to the dilemma offer an easy solution, I suggest that my dilemma may be dialectically welcome to unitarians.
